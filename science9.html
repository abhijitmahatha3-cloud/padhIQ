Matter in Our Surroundings

States of Matter · Sublimation · Evaporation · Latent Heat
⚛️
What is Matter?

Anything that has mass and occupies space is called matter. All physical things — water, air, stone, chair — are matter.

⚛️
Characteristics of Particles of Matter
  • Particles have spaces between them (intermolecular spaces)
  • Particles are constantly moving (kinetic energy)
  • Particles attract each other (intermolecular forces)
  • Particles are extremely tiny — not visible to naked eye

Changes of State
Solid →(Melting)→ Liquid →(Vaporisation)→ Gas Gas →(Condensation)→ Liquid →(Freezing)→ Solid Solid →(Sublimation)→ Gas (directly)
  • Melting Point: Temperature at which solid converts to liquid. Ice = 0°C
  • Boiling Point: Temperature at which liquid converts to gas. Water = 100°C at 1 atm
  • Sublimation: Direct solid → gas conversion. Examples: Camphor, Dry Ice, Naphthalene, Iodine
  • Deposition: Direct gas → solid. Example: Frost formation
🧊
Effect of Pressure on State
  • Increasing pressure can convert gas to liquid (liquefaction)
  • Dry ice (solid CO₂) — CO₂ is liquefied under high pressure
  • LPG — butane and propane stored as liquid under pressure
  • Increasing pressure on ice at 0°C converts it to water
💧
Evaporation

Evaporation is a surface phenomenon — liquid converts to gas below boiling point, only from the surface.

  • Evaporation increases with: ↑ temperature, ↑ surface area, ↑ wind speed, ↓ humidity
  • Evaporation causes cooling — sweating cools body, earthen pot cools water
  • Evaporation ≠ Boiling: Evaporation is surface only, at any temperature. Boiling occurs throughout, at a specific temperature.
💧
Latent Heat
  • Latent Heat of Fusion: Heat required to convert 1 kg solid to liquid at melting point (no temperature change). Ice: 336,000 J/kg
  • Latent Heat of Vaporisation: Heat required to convert 1 kg liquid to gas at boiling point. Water: 2,260,000 J/kg
Steam causes more severe burns than boiling water — steam carries extra latent heat of vaporisation (2260 kJ/kg) which is released on skin.
Pressure cooker: Increased pressure raises boiling point above 100°C → food cooks faster. Mountains: Low pressure → boiling point below 100°C → food takes longer.

Is Matter Around Us Pure?

Mixtures · Solutions · Colloids · Tyndall Effect
🧪
Pure Substances vs Mixtures
  • Pure Substance: Fixed composition, definite properties. E.g., Water, Gold, NaCl, O₂
  • Mixture: Two or more substances mixed but not chemically combined. Properties are a combination of components.
🧪
Types of Mixtures
  • Homogeneous Mixture (Solution): Uniform composition throughout. E.g., salt water, air, vinegar, alloys
  • Heterogeneous Mixture: Non-uniform, components visible. E.g., soil, salad, gunpowder, muddy water
🧪
Solutions

A solution is a homogeneous mixture. Solute (dissolved, smaller amount) + Solvent (dissolving medium, larger amount)

Concentration (%) = (Mass of Solute / Mass of Solution) × 100 Mass of Solution = Mass of Solute + Mass of Solvent
  • Unsaturated: Can dissolve more solute at given temperature
  • Saturated: Cannot dissolve more at given temperature
  • Supersaturated: More solute than a saturated solution (unstable)

🧪
Tyndall Effect

Scattering of light by colloidal particles making the path of light visible. Examples: headlights in fog, sunlight through trees, beam of torch in smoke.

⚗️
Separation Techniques
  • Evaporation: Separates dissolved solid from liquid (salt from water)
  • Filtration: Separates insoluble solid from liquid (sand from water)
  • Centrifugation: Separates based on density using spinning (butter from milk, blood cells from plasma)
  • Chromatography: Separates coloured components/dyes based on solubility (ink colours, plant pigments)
  • Simple Distillation: Separates miscible liquids with large boiling point difference (water from salt solution)
  • Fractional Distillation: Separates miscible liquids with close boiling points (alcohol+water, petroleum refining, air separation)
  • Sublimation: Separates sublimable substance from non-sublimable (ammonium chloride from salt)
  • Magnetic Separation: Separates magnetic from non-magnetic (iron from sulfur)
📌
Elements and Compounds
  • Element: Pure substance made of one type of atom. Cannot be broken down further by chemical methods. 118 elements known. E.g., H, O, Fe, Au, Na
  • Compound: Two or more elements chemically combined in a definite ratio. Has different properties from constituent elements. E.g., H₂O, CO₂, NaCl, HCl
Water (H₂O) — H₂ is flammable, O₂ supports combustion, but H₂O puts out fire! Compounds have completely different properties from their elements.

Atoms and Molecules

Atomic Mass · Mole Concept · Chemical Formulae
⚗️
Laws of Chemical Combination
  • Law of Conservation of Mass (Lavoisier, 1774): In a chemical reaction, the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products. Mass is neither created nor destroyed.
  • Law of Constant Proportions (Proust, 1799): A pure chemical compound always contains the same elements in the same definite ratio by mass. E.g., Water always has H:O = 1:8 by mass, regardless of its source.
⚗️
Atoms

An atom is the smallest particle of an element that can take part in a chemical reaction. Atoms are extremely small — radius ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m. John Dalton proposed the atomic theory.

  • Atomic Mass Unit (amu / u): 1/12th the mass of a carbon-12 atom. 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg

⚗️
Molecules

The smallest particle of a substance that can exist independently and has all the properties of that substance.

  • Monoatomic: Noble gases — He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe (stable as single atoms)
  • Diatomic: H₂, O₂, N₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂ (HONClBrIF)
  • Triatomic: O₃ (ozone), H₂O, CO₂
  • Polyatomic: P₄ (4 atoms), S₈ (8 atoms)
⚗️
Formulae and Valency
  • Valency: Combining capacity of an atom. H=1, O=2, N=3, C=4, Na=1, K=1, Ca=2, Mg=2, Al=3, Cl=1, Fe=2or3
  • To write formula: cross-multiply valencies (criss-cross rule)
Example: Magnesium Oxide Mg (valency 2) + O (valency 2) → MgO (valencies cancel, so 1:1) Example: Aluminium Oxide Al (valency 3) + O (valency 2) → Al₂O₃ (cross multiply: Al gets 2, O gets 3)
⚗️
Mole Concept

A mole is the SI unit for amount of substance. 1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's Number).

Avogadro's Number (Nₐ) = 6.022 × 10²³ Molar Mass = Atomic/Molecular mass in grams Number of moles (n) = Given mass (m) / Molar Mass (M) Number of particles = n × 6.022 × 10²³ Volume at STP (for gases) = n × 22.4 L
1 mole of ANY substance contains 6.022 × 10²³ particles. 1 mole of H₂O = 18 g = 6.022 × 10²³ molecules. 1 mole of Fe = 56 g = 6.022 × 10²³ atoms.

Structure of the Atom

Thomson · Rutherford · Bohr · Isotopes · Isobars
⚛️
Discovery of Subatomic Particles
  • Electron: J.J. Thomson (1897) — cathode ray tube. Charge: −1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C, Mass: 9.1×10⁻³¹ kg
  • Proton: E. Goldstein (1886) — canal rays (anode rays). Charge: +1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C, Mass: 1.67×10⁻²⁷ kg
  • Neutron: James Chadwick (1932). Charge: 0, Mass: slightly > proton
⚗️
Atomic Models
  • Thomson's Plum Pudding Model (1904): Atom = sphere of positive charge with electrons embedded like plums in pudding. Drawback: Could not explain Rutherford's scattering results.
  • Rutherford's Nuclear Model (1911): Gold foil experiment → most particles passed through, few deflected, very few bounced back. Conclusion: Atom has tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus; electrons orbit it; most of atom is empty space. Drawback: accelerating electrons should spiral into nucleus (unstable).
  • Bohr's Model (1913): Electrons orbit in specific circular paths (shells/orbits) with fixed energy. No energy emitted while in orbit. Energy emitted/absorbed only when electrons jump between orbits.
⚗️
Electron Distribution (Bohr-Bury Rules)

Cell Organelles and Functions
  • Plasma Membrane (Cell Membrane): Selectively permeable phospholipid bilayer. Controls entry/exit of substances. Present in ALL cells.
  • Cell Wall: Rigid outer layer in plants (cellulose), fungi (chitin), bacteria (peptidoglycan). Gives shape and protection.
  • Nucleus: "Control centre" — contains chromosomes (DNA), controls all cell activities. Has nuclear membrane, nucleoplasm, nucleolus, chromatin.
  • Mitochondria: "Powerhouse of the cell" — site of aerobic respiration. Produces ATP. Has its own DNA and ribosomes (semi-autonomous).
  • Chloroplast: Site of photosynthesis. Contains chlorophyll (green pigment). Has its own DNA. Found only in plant cells and algae.
  • Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER): Network of membranes. Rough ER (with ribosomes) — protein synthesis and transport. Smooth ER — lipid and steroid synthesis.
  • Ribosomes: Smallest organelle. Site of protein synthesis. Found on rough ER and free in cytoplasm. Present in all cells (prokaryotic too).
  • Golgi Apparatus (Golgi Body): "Post office of cell" — receives proteins from ER, modifies, packages, and dispatches them. Forms lysosomes.
  • Lysosome: "Suicide bag" — contains digestive enzymes (hydrolases). Breaks down old organelles, food particles, invading bacteria.
  • Vacuole: Storage organelle. Large central vacuole in plants maintains turgor pressure. Food vacuoles in unicellular organisms.
  • Centrosome/Centriole: Helps form spindle fibres during cell division. Present in animal cells and lower plants.
⚗️
Diffusion and Osmosis
  • Diffusion: Movement of particles from high concentration to low concentration. No membrane required. E.g., smell spreading in room, CO₂ entering leaf.
  • Osmosis: Movement of water molecules across a semipermeable membrane from low solute concentration (hypotonic) to high solute concentration (hypertonic).
  • Hypotonic solution: Solute conc less than cell — water enters, cell swells (turgid)
  • Hypertonic solution: Solute conc more than cell — water leaves, cell shrinks (plasmolysis)
  • Isotonic solution: Equal solute concentration — no net water movement
Mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own DNA and ribosomes — supporting the Endosymbiotic Theory that they were once free-living bacteria.

Tissues

Meristematic · Permanent · Epithelial · Muscular
🌿
What is a Tissue?

A tissue is a group of similar cells that work together to perform a specific function. Division of labour at the cellular level — allows organisms to be more efficient.

🌿
Plant Tissues

A. Meristematic Tissue — actively dividing cells, responsible for growth

  • Apical meristem: tip of roots and shoots → increases length
  • Lateral meristem (Cambium): sides of stem/root → increases girth/width
  • Intercalary meristem: base of leaves, internodes → regeneration after grazing

B. Permanent Tissue — cells that have stopped dividing, differentiated

Simple Permanent Tissue:

  • Parenchyma: Thin-walled, loosely packed, large vacuoles. Stores food and water. Site of photosynthesis in leaves (chlorenchyma). Most common plant tissue.
  • Collenchyma: Irregularly thickened walls at corners. Provides mechanical support and flexibility to young stems. Found in leaf stalks.
  • Sclerenchyma: Dead cells with very thick, lignified walls. Provides hardness and rigidity. Found in coconut husk, jute fibres, seed coats.

Complex Permanent Tissue:

  • Xylem: Transports water and minerals from roots to leaves (upward transport). Components: Tracheids, Vessels (main), Xylem fibres, Xylem parenchyma. Vessels are dead at maturity.
  • Phloem: Transports food (sugar/glucose) from leaves to all parts (bidirectional transport). Components: Sieve tubes, Companion cells (alive), Phloem fibres, Phloem parenchyma.
🌿
Animal Tissues

1. Epithelial Tissue — covers and lines body surfaces

  • Squamous epithelium: flat cells, skin, blood vessels, alveoli
  • Cuboidal epithelium: cube-shaped, kidney tubules, salivary glands
  • Columnar epithelium: tall cells, intestinal lining (absorption)
  • Ciliated epithelium: columnar with cilia, respiratory tract (removes dust)
  • Glandular epithelium: secretion (sweat glands, gastric glands)

2. Connective Tissue — connects, supports, and fills spaces

  • Blood: liquid (plasma) matrix — oxygen transport, immunity
  • Bone: rigid matrix (calcium phosphate) — structural support
  • Cartilage: semi-rigid matrix — tip of nose, ear, between vertebrae
  • Areolar: loosely packed fibres — fills spaces between organs
  • Adipose: fat storage — insulation, cushioning
  • Tendon: connects muscle to bone (strong, inelastic)
  • Ligament: connects bone to bone (strong, slightly elastic)

Crop Improvement
  • Hybridisation: Crossing two different varieties/species to get desired traits — higher yield, disease resistance, drought resistance

  • Manure: Organic material from decomposed plant/animal waste. Improves soil texture, water-holding capacity, micro-organisms. Types: Compost, green manure, vermicompost, farmyard manure

Cropping Patterns
  • Mixed Cropping: Two or more crops grown simultaneously on same land. Reduces risk — if one fails, other survives. E.g., Wheat + Gram, Soybean + Maize
  • Inter-cropping: Two or more crops grown simultaneously in alternate rows. Maximises use of space, nutrients. E.g., Soybean + Maize in alternate rows
  • Crop Rotation: Growing different crops alternately on same land in different seasons. Prevents soil nutrient depletion. Legumes (fix N₂) alternated with cereal crops.

Crop Protection
  • Weeds: Unwanted plants competing for nutrients/light. Control: Weeding (physical removal), herbicides (chemical)

  • Diseases: Caused by fungi, bacteria, viruses. Control: Fungicides, bactericides, crop rotation
  • Storage: Silos (large storage) and granaries. Protect from: moisture, pests, rats. Use: fumigants, proper drying

Motion

Distance · Velocity · Acceleration · Equations · Graphs
⚗️
Basic Definitions
  • Distance: Total path length covered. Scalar (no direction). Always positive or zero.
  • Displacement: Shortest distance from initial to final position, with direction. Vector quantity. Can be zero, positive, or negative.
  • Speed: Distance / Time. Scalar. Unit: m/s
  • Velocity: Displacement / Time. Vector. Unit: m/s
  • Acceleration: Rate of change of velocity. a = (v−u)/t. Vector. Unit: m/s²
⚗️
Types of Motion
  • Uniform motion: Equal distances in equal time intervals. Speed is constant. Acceleration = 0.
  • Non-uniform motion: Unequal distances in equal time intervals. Speed varies.
  • Uniform acceleration: Velocity changes by equal amounts in equal time intervals.
⚗️
Equations of Uniformly Accelerated Motion
v = u + at ← First Equation s = ut + ½at² ← Second Equation v² = u² + 2as ← Third Equation s = (u + v)t / 2 ← Alternative form Where: u = initial velocity, v = final velocity a = acceleration, t = time, s = displacement
⚗️
Graphical Representation
  • Distance-Time Graph:
    • Slope = Speed. Horizontal line = at rest. Straight diagonal line = uniform speed. Curved line = non-uniform speed (acceleration).
  • Velocity-Time Graph:
    • Slope = Acceleration. Area under graph = Displacement.
    • Horizontal line = uniform velocity (zero acceleration)
    • Straight sloping line = uniform acceleration
    • Triangle area under graph = ½ × base × height = displacement
⚗️
Uniform Circular Motion

Motion along a circle at constant speed. Even though speed is constant, velocity is not (direction keeps changing). Therefore it IS accelerated motion — centripetal acceleration directed towards centre.

Speed in circular motion = Circumference / Time period v = 2πr / T
If a body completes a full circle and returns to start: Displacement = 0, Distance = circumference. This is a common exam trap!
Negative acceleration is called deceleration or retardation (vehicle slowing down, brakes applied).

Force and Laws of Motion

Newton's Laws · Inertia · Momentum · Conservation
⚡
Force

A force is a push or pull that can change the state of rest or motion of an object, change its speed, direction, or shape. Unit: Newton (N). Vector quantity. 1 N = 1 kg·m/s²

⚡
Newton's First Law — Law of Inertia

An object remains in its state of rest or uniform motion in a straight line unless acted upon by an unbalanced external force.

  • Inertia: The tendency of an object to resist change in its state of motion. Depends ONLY on mass.
  • Greater mass = greater inertia (heavier objects are harder to start/stop/change direction)
  • Examples: Passenger jerks forward when bus brakes; dust falls from carpet when beaten; coin falls into glass when card is flicked.
⚡
Newton's Second Law
F = ma Force = Mass × Acceleration Unit: 1 Newton = 1 kg × 1 m/s² Also: F = Δp/Δt (Rate of change of momentum)
⚡
Momentum
p = mv Momentum = Mass × Velocity Unit: kg·m/s (Vector quantity) Impulse = F × t = Δp (change in momentum)
⚡
Newton's Third Law

"For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. Both forces act simultaneously but on DIFFERENT objects."

  • Rocket: gas expelled down (action) → rocket moves up (reaction)
  • Gun recoil: bullet forward (action) → gun backward (reaction)
  • Swimming: push water backward (action) → move forward (reaction)
  • Walking: foot pushes ground backward (action) → ground pushes foot forward (reaction)
⚗️
Law of Conservation of Momentum

Total momentum of an isolated system remains constant when no external force acts.

m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ (Total momentum before collision = Total momentum after)
Impulse explains safety devices: padded dashboards, airbags, helmets increase the time of impact → reduce force (F = Δp/t). Same change in momentum, but over longer time = less force.

Gravitation

Universal Law · g · Free Fall · Buoyancy · Archimedes
⚗️
Universal Law of Gravitation

Every object in the universe attracts every other object with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.

F = G × m₁ × m₂ / r² G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²·kg⁻² (Universal Gravitational Constant) m₁, m₂ = masses of two objects, r = distance between their centres
⚗️
Acceleration due to Gravity (g)
g = GM_Earth / R²_Earth = 9.8 m/s² Weight W = mg On Moon: g_moon = 1.6 m/s² (≈ 1/6 of Earth)
  • g is maximum at poles, minimum at equator
  • g decreases as altitude increases (moving away from Earth)
  • g decreases as depth increases (moving towards Earth's centre)
  • Mass is same everywhere; weight changes with location
🌍
Free Fall
  • Object falls under gravity alone (no air resistance) → free fall
  • All objects fall with same acceleration g, regardless of mass (Galileo)
  • During free fall: apparent weight = 0 (weightlessness) — astronauts in orbit, person in falling elevator
For free fall (u = 0, a = g = 9.8 m/s²): v = gt h = ½gt² v² = 2gh Time to fall from height h: t = √(2h/g)
🌍
Buoyancy and Archimedes' Principle
  • Buoyancy: Upward force exerted by a fluid on an immersed object
  • Archimedes' Principle: When an object is fully or partially immersed in a fluid, it experiences a buoyant force equal to the weight of fluid displaced by it.
  • Object floats if buoyant force ≥ weight (density of object ≤ density of fluid)
  • Object sinks if buoyant force < weight (density of object > density of fluid)
Buoyant force = Weight of fluid displaced = ρ_fluid × V_displaced × g Relative Density (Specific Gravity) = Density of substance / Density of water (1000 kg/m³)
A ship floats even though steel is denser than water because the hollow ship displaces a large volume of water — the weight of water displaced equals the ship's weight.

Work and Energy

Work · KE · PE · Conservation of Energy · Power
🔋
Work

Work is done when a force causes displacement of an object in the direction of force. Work is a scalar quantity.

W = F × s × cos θ W = Work (Joules, J), F = Force (N), s = displacement (m) θ = angle between force and displacement direction θ = 0°: W = Fs (maximum positive work) θ = 90°: W = 0 (no work — force ⊥ displacement, e.g., carrying bag horizontally) θ = 180°: W = −Fs (negative work — friction opposing motion)
🔋
Energy

Energy is the capacity to do work. Scalar quantity. Unit: Joule (J). 1 calorie = 4.2 J

  • Kinetic Energy (KE): Energy due to motion
KE = ½mv² m = mass (kg), v = velocity (m/s)
  • Potential Energy (PE): Energy stored due to position or configuration
Gravitational PE = mgh m = mass, g = 9.8 m/s², h = height above reference
  • Other forms: Chemical energy (food, fuel), Heat energy, Electrical energy, Nuclear energy, Light energy, Sound energy
🔋
Work-Energy Theorem
Net Work done = Change in Kinetic Energy W_net = ΔKE = ½mv² − ½mu²
⚗️
Law of Conservation of Energy

Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be converted from one form to another. Total energy of an isolated system remains constant.

At any point: KE + PE = Constant (Total Mechanical Energy) At highest point: KE = 0, PE = mgh (maximum) At lowest point: KE = ½mv² (maximum), PE = 0
🔋
Power
Power P = Work / Time = W/t Unit: Watt (W) = Joule/second (J/s) 1 kW = 1000 W 1 Horsepower (HP) = 746 W Commercial unit: 1 kWh (kilowatt-hour) = 3.6 × 10⁶ J Electric energy consumed: E = P × t (in kWh)
1 unit of electricity on your electricity bill = 1 kWh = 1000 W used for 1 hour = 3,600,000 Joules.

⚗️
Production and Propagation of Sound
  • Sound is produced by vibrating objects (vocal cords, strings, tuning forks)
  • Sound is a mechanical longitudinal wave — needs material medium (solid, liquid, gas) to travel
  • Sound CANNOT travel in vacuum — bell in vacuum jar experiment
  • In longitudinal waves: particles vibrate parallel to wave propagation direction
  • Sound creates alternating compressions (high pressure) and rarefactions (low pressure)
🚀
Speed of Sound
Solids > Liquids > Gases Sound in air at 25°C ≈ 346 m/s Sound in water ≈ 1500 m/s Sound in steel ≈ 5100 m/s Speed increases with temperature in air
🔊
Characteristics of Sound
v = f × λ (wave equation) v = velocity (m/s), f = frequency (Hz), λ = wavelength (m) Time period T = 1/f
  • Frequency (Hz): Vibrations per second → determines pitch. High frequency = high pitch (shrill)
  • Amplitude: Maximum displacement of particles → determines loudness
  • Wavelength (λ): Distance between two consecutive compressions
  • Pitch: Shrillness/Highness of sound. Depends on frequency.
  • Loudness (dB): Depends on amplitude of vibration. Measured in decibels.
  • Timbre (Quality): Makes different instruments sound different even at same pitch/loudness

Reflection of Sound — Echo and Reverberation
  • Echo: Distinct reflected sound. Conditions: (1) Minimum distance from reflector = 17.2 m (2) Reflecting surface must be large (3) Speed of sound = 346 m/s
  • Reverberation: Multiple reflections causing persistence of sound. Problematic in large halls. Reduced by sound-absorbing materials (carpets, curtains, rough walls, perforated tiles).
Echo: d = v × t / 2 (Sound travels to obstacle and back, so divide by 2)
